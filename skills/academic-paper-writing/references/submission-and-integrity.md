# Submission And Integrity

Use this file when the user needs help with:

- journal or conference selection
- a cover letter
- submission readiness checks
- a response to reviewers
- authorship, acknowledgments, conflicts of interest, or AI-use disclosure
- publication ethics or reporting-guideline questions

## Journal and venue fit

Choose the venue before fine-tuning the manuscript style.

Check:

- aims and scope
- audience
- article types accepted
- word limits and figure limits
- ethical standards
- review speed or publication model if relevant

Do not recommend a journal only by prestige or impact metrics. Fit, ethics, and audience are usually more important than raw rank.

Watch for predatory or unreliable venues. If the user is unsure, prioritize publisher transparency, indexing, editorial standards, and visible publication-ethics policies.

## Submission package

Before submission, verify:

- manuscript matches the journal's structure and formatting instructions
- abstract format matches the venue
- keywords are present if required
- figures, tables, and supplementary files are named consistently
- word count and figure/table counts are accurate
- funding, acknowledgments, disclosures, and ethics statements are present where needed

If the venue gives explicit instructions, follow those instructions even when they differ from generic norms.

## Cover letter

Write cover letters as brief editorial positioning documents.

Usually include:

- manuscript title and article type
- the problem or question the paper addresses
- the main contribution or finding
- why the paper fits the journal's audience
- any required declarations, such as originality, exclusivity, or conflicts, if the journal asks for them

Avoid:

- pasting the abstract verbatim
- excessive jargon
- a long methods summary
- hype language
- letters longer than roughly one page unless the venue asks for more

## Responses to reviewers

Treat reviewer responses as part of the scholarly argument, not as damage control.

Default process:

1. Read all comments before drafting replies.
2. Pause long enough to separate emotional reaction from editorial judgment.
3. Group comments into major revisions, minor revisions, and misunderstandings.
4. Draft a point-by-point response.
5. For each comment, state what changed and where, or give a polite evidence-based reason for not changing it.

Recommended reply pattern:

- Thank the reviewer for the specific point.
- State the action taken.
- Quote or summarize the revised wording when helpful.
- Point to the new section, paragraph, table, or page number if known.

When disagreeing:

- stay calm
- defend with evidence, method, or scope
- do not argue from ego or intention

## Authorship and acknowledgments

Use a conservative standard for authorship.

For biomedical and many adjacent fields, the ICMJE four-part test is a strong default reference:

- substantial contribution to the work
- drafting or critical intellectual revision
- final approval
- accountability for the work

Do not assign authorship only for funding, supervision, proofreading, or language polishing. Those belong in acknowledgments unless the field follows a different written policy.

## Conflicts, funding, and transparency

Disclose relationships and activities that could reasonably affect how the work is interpreted. Disclose funding sources accurately. Do not imply support that did not materially contribute to the work.

## AI use, plagiarism, and citation integrity

Never use AI to generate fake scholarship.

Rules:

- AI tools are not authors.
- Humans remain responsible for accuracy, originality, attribution, and disclosure.
- If AI was used in a way the venue requires to be disclosed, say so clearly in the appropriate place.
- Do not paraphrase sources so closely that the wording remains effectively copied.
- Do not cite works you have not actually verified.

If the user asks for references without a source set, offer search terms or a placeholder bibliography structure instead of invented citations.

## Reporting guidelines

If the paper belongs to a study type with a recognized reporting standard, use it as a checklist while writing.

Examples:

- PRISMA for systematic reviews
- STROBE for observational studies

If the field is health or clinical research, look for the specific reporting guideline that matches the design before finalizing the manuscript.
